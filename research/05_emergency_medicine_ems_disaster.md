## 1. Emergency medicine as a specialty in Chile

### 1.1 Before the specialty: who ran the EDs

Until the 1990s, and in most of the country still in 2005, Chilean EDs were staffed without emergency physicians [K/E]:
- **Médicos de turno** (shift doctors) under **Ley 15.076**, typically on a **28-hour week built around one 24-hour shift** [K]. Many were general practitioners who spent whole careers in the "urgencia" without specialist certification: the classic *médico de urgencia*.
- **The surgeon ran the shift.** In big hospital EDs (HUAP, Barros Luco, Sótero del Río, San Juan de Dios), the **jefe de turno** was usually a **general surgeon**; the ED was split into a **surgical side** (trauma, "cirugía") and a **medical side** ("medicina", run by internists or GPs), with separate paediatric and obstetric emergencies [K].
- **Residents (becados)** of surgery, internal medicine, traumatology and anaesthesia covered shifts as part of training, supervised loosely at night [K].
- **Internos** (seventh-year students) took histories, sutured, ran ECGs and blood gases, and were often the first person a patient saw [K].
- In provincial and rural hospitals, the **EDF** doctors (Etapa de Destinación y Formación, the "médicos generales de zona" system dating from the 1950s) covered emergencies alone for years before specializing [K].
- Primary-care out-of-hours urgent care (**SAPU**, from 1990) was staffed by GPs [K].

### 1.3 How many emergency physicians?

No reliable counts were retrieved. The following are **[E] estimates**, reasoned from the start date (1994), small early cohorts, the 2004 relaunch, 14 programmes by ~2023, and certification of experienced ED doctors by CONACEM:

| Year | Certified EM specialists in Chile (estimate) | What it feels like |
|---|---|---|
| **2005** | **≈ 30–80** | Everyone knows everyone; most work in a handful of Santiago EDs and teaching programmes; most EDs in Chile have none |
| **2015** | **≈ 250–450** | Every big Santiago ED has some; private clinics start advertising "urgenciólogos"; the regions still lack them |
| **2025** | **≈ 700–1,200** | Normal in big EDs and private clinics; still a recognized shortage in the regions; many ED shifts still covered by GPs and foreign doctors |

The register of specialists (Registro Nacional de Prestadores Individuales, Superintendencia de Salud) is the place to check real numbers [K].

### 1.4 Certification and societies

- **CONACEM** (1984) certifies specialists, including by an **exam route for doctors trained in service** (years of ED work plus supervised assessment) [K]. In the 1990s and 2000s that route let veteran *médicos de urgencia* become certified, which irritated some residency graduates [E].
- **SOCHIMU (Sociedad Chilena de Medicina de Urgencia)** is the specialty society; it runs congresses and courses [K]. Its **founding year could not be verified**. Neighbouring societies: **SOCHIMI** (Sociedad Chilena de Medicina Intensiva), the **Sociedad de Cirujanos de Chile**, the Sociedad Chilena de Cardiología [K].
- Public figures documented in the literature (named only for documented facts): **Leoncio Tay U.** and **Carlos Becerra V.** of SAMU Metropolitano wrote the 2011 review "Ley de urgencia y riesgo vital" [V]; **Pablo Aguilera** (PUC Emergency Medicine Section) co-authored the 2020 overview of EM in Chile [V]; **Manuel Ipinza Riveros** wrote the 2007 history of the specialty's origins [R].

### 1.5 The culture clash

[K/E: composite from the structure above and common professional experience; not a sourced account]
- **Surgeons**: the trauma bay was theirs. To many, the urgenciólogo was "a triage doctor with pretensions". Friction points: who leads the trauma resuscitation, who does the **FAST/eFAST** ultrasound, who places the chest drain, who decides on the OR, and whether an EM doctor should do a **resuscitative thoracotomy**.
- **Internists**: who "owns" the sick medical patient waiting for a bed; who writes the admission; whether the ED holds patients for 30 hours.
- **Anaesthetists**: the airway. **Rapid-sequence intubation** by EM doctors was contested in the early 2000s and normal by the 2010s.
- **Intensivists**: the "ICU in the ED" when no bed exists; vasopressors and ventilators in the hallway.
- **The old médicos de urgencia**: experienced, unaccredited, sometimes brilliant, sometimes dangerous; resentment both ways.
- **Hospital management**: EM specialists brought protocols, categorization, metrics (door-to-needle, waiting times), all of which expose management failures.

### 2.2 SAMU Metropolitano

- **Origins** [V]: a French medical mission from the **Hôpital de la Pitié-Salpêtrière and the SAMU de Paris**, sponsored by the French Embassy, worked with the **Servicio de Salud Metropolitano Central** and the **Hospital de Urgencia Asistencia Pública (HUAP)**. **SAMU Metropolitano** was inaugurated in the HUAP courtyard on **20 December 1995**; the **131** number was created the same year. The first Chilean SAMU had opened in **Viña del Mar in 1994** (some sources say its roots go back to 1993 at Hospital Gustavo Fricke).
- **Growth** [V]: the HUAP's initiative and the **Rescate Área Norte** were folded into a **MINSAL project in 2003**; the **Servicio de Salud Metropolitano Sur Oriente** joined in **2005**. SAMU remained under the HUAP until **1 January 2017**, when a MINSAL decree created a separate metropolitan pre-hospital care centre.
- **The French model** [V/R]: a **regulation centre** where calls are answered by dispatchers and a **médico regulador** (regulating physician) decides the response: advice, a basic unit, an advanced unit, a physician. For advanced ambulances, regulating physicians coordinate dispatch; basic ambulances are sent after authorization by a supervisor and dispatcher [R]. The regulation centre of SAMU RM is **the largest pre-hospital system in Chile**, receiving **2,500–3,500 calls a day** on 131 (2019 thesis) [V].
- **Norma General Técnica N° 17** (Res. Exenta 338, **2 March 2005**) defined the national SAMU system [R].
- **Mobile units** [R]: **M1 / AEB** (basic): crew of **two**, driver plus técnico paramédico, both trained in pre-hospital care. **M2 / AEA** (advanced): crew of **three**, driver plus TENS plus **reanimador** (a nurse, midwife or other trained professional) or a physician. Critical inter-hospital transfers may run with four. **"M3"** is used for advanced units carrying a physician [K, terminology varies].
- **Staff roles** (MINSAL's own phrasing in 2019: "médicos, reanimadores y paramédicos", "the visible face of SAMU") [R].
- **Under stress**: during the **October 2019** emergency, calls to 131 **doubled** [R].
- **Known weaknesses** [K/E]: few units per inhabitant compared with European systems; long waits in the periphery and in winter; calls triaged out by the regulator, which families resent; ambulances stoned or attacked in some poblaciones and during protests; crews wearing helmets and vests after 2019.

### 2.3 Bomberos

- **Volunteer firefighters**, unpaid, organized in **cuerpos** (city corps) made of numbered **compañías** [K]. Chile's first corps was Valparaíso's (**1851**); the **Cuerpo de Bomberos de Santiago** was founded in **December 1863** after the **Iglesia de la Compañía fire** (8 Dec 1863), which killed some 2,000 people, most of them women [K]. Several Santiago compañías were founded by immigrant communities (French, German, Italian, Spanish, British and others) and keep their traditions, languages on the door and rivalries [K].
- **Role**: fire, **vehicle extrication** (rescate vehicular), **hazmat**, structural collapse and **USAR**, water rescue. In Santiago they **do not normally transport patients**: SAMU or private ambulances do; bomberos stabilize and extricate [K/E].
- **People**: lawyers, students, mechanics, doctors and many TENS volunteer; tradition passes in families. A Founder who is himself a *bombero* (many doctors were) would have instant credibility on scene [E].
- **Funding**: state subsidies, municipal support, public appeals, insurers' contributions [K].

### 2.4 Carabineros and PDI

- Carabineros are **often first on scene** at traffic accidents, shootings and stabbings [K]. They write the **parte** (police report) needed by SOAP insurers and prosecutors, and the **SIAT** (Sección de Investigación de Accidentes de Tránsito) investigates serious crashes [K].
- Historically, injured people were sometimes brought to the ED **in police cars or by passers-by** rather than ambulance, especially in poblaciones and at night [K/E].
- Carabineros' **air police** (Prefectura Aeropolicial) and the **FACh** have flown medical evacuations in mountain accidents, disasters and COVID-19 [K].
- After 2019, police–health relations in EDs became tense: detained protesters brought in for "constatación de lesiones" (forensic injury documentation), health workers reporting injuries by state agents, and police guarding wounded detainees [K/E].

|---|---|---|---|

| **Rescate Alemana** (Clínica Alemana) | Clinic-owned | **Integrated air-ground rescue unit**, 24 h, medical and paramedical crews; in some agreements ground transfer within urban Santiago is free and air transfer free only in vital risk | [R] |

**Market logic** [E]: subscription rescue is strongest in the eastern comunas (Las Condes, Vitacura, Providencia, Ñuñoa, La Reina, Lo Barnechea) and in companies. Each clinic-owned service is also a **feeder**: the ambulance brings the patient to its owner's ED. An independent network that delivers to "the nearest appropriate ED" is either more ethical or less profitable, depending on how the DM plays it. Under the Ley de Urgencia, whoever receives a vital emergency **cannot ask for guarantees**, so the feeder logic is about which **insurer** the patient has, not cash at the door.

**Word trap**: in Chile, **"paramédico" means the TENS** (a 2-year nursing technician), not a North-American-style paramedic with independent advanced practice [K]. The **reanimador** is the advanced provider, usually a nurse.

### 2.7 Air ambulances and heliports

- **Clínica Alemana** operates an air-ground rescue unit [R]. Major private clinics (Alemana, CLC) and some institutional hospitals have **heliports** [K/E: check each site]. MINSAL lists **DS 83 (2010)** among the rules for air transport of patients [R].
- Long-distance transfers to Santiago (from the north, the far south, Easter Island) use **fixed-wing air ambulances**, private or FACh [K].
- Helicopter EMS inside Santiago is rare and expensive; smog, winter fog and wind limit flights [E].

### 2.8 Response times

No validated figures were retrieved. **[E]**: SAMU in central and eastern Santiago, **15–30 minutes** for priority calls; peripheral comunas and winter peaks, **30–60+ minutes**, with long waits for low-priority calls; private rescue in the eastern comunas advertises **~15–25 minutes**. Traffic on Vicuña Mackenna, Gran Avenida, the Alameda and the Costanera at rush hour is the main enemy; so are winter rain, protests and closed streets.

## 3. The big emergency departments of Santiago and their culture

### 3.1 The public emergency pathway

**[K]** Patient → (SAPU / SAR for low complexity) → **UEH** of the hospital of his Servicio de Salud → admission ("cama") or transfer. Adult, paediatric and obstetric emergencies are usually separate units. Ambulances (SAMU) take critical patients to the appropriate UEH; trauma and burns to the **HUAP**.

### 3.2 The Posta Central (Hospital de Urgencia Asistencia Pública, HUAP)

- **What it is** [K]: Santiago's historic emergency hospital in Santiago Centro (on Avenida Portugal, a few blocks south of the Alameda, close to Plaza Italia), the city's **main adult trauma centre** and the **national adult burns reference** ("gran quemado"). Its name comes from the old **Asistencia Pública**, the city's municipal emergency service, whose roots go back to the late 19th century [V for "origins to late 1800s" via SAMU's history].
- **SAMU's cradle** [V]: SAMU Metropolitano was born in its courtyard (1995) and depended on it until 2017.
- **Lore** [K/E]: every Chilean doctor has a Posta story. It is where the city's violence ends up: knife fights from Bellavista bars, stabbings from Estación Central, motorcyclists from the Alameda, burned workers, people injured in protests (in the 1980s and again in 2019, when it sat less than a kilometre from the epicentre at Plaza Italia). Overcrowded, underfunded, legendary for surgical skill and for patients on stretchers in the corridors. Outside: the *completos* (hot-dog) carts, families waiting on the pavement, carabineros guarding detainees, journalists on big nights.
- **Game use**: the natural rival, ally and benchmark for a private stabilization network in central Santiago. Its staff will moonlight for the Founder, refer patients, resent him, or all three.

### 3.3 Hospital del Trabajador (ACHS)

[K] The Asociación Chilena de Seguridad's flagship hospital (Ramón Carnicer, Providencia, by Parque Bustamante; about a kilometre from Plaza Italia). Specialized in **occupational trauma**: crush injuries, falls from height, hand surgery, spinal cord injury, **burns** and rehabilitation. Serves workers of ACHS-affiliated companies, including **commuting accidents**. Highly organized, well equipped, protocol-driven: a model of what money and a single payer can do. Not a general public ED.

### 3.4 Complejo Asistencial Dr. Sótero del Río

[K] In **Puente Alto**, head hospital of the SSM Sur Oriente, serving a huge, largely working-class population (Puente Alto, La Florida, La Pintana, Pirque, San José de Maipo). One of the **busiest EDs in Chile**; a major PUC teaching site [K]. Gunshot and stab wounds, falls from social-housing blocks, road trauma from Avenida Concha y Toro and Vicuña Mackenna, winter respiratory surges. Chronic overcrowding and hallway patients are part of its identity; staff take pride in surviving it.

### 3.5 Hospital Padre Hurtado

[K] Opened **1998** in **San Ramón** (SSM Sur Oriente), designed as a modern, management-innovative public hospital; teaching campus of the **Universidad del Desarrollo / Clínica Alemana**. Busy ED serving La Granja, San Ramón and La Pintana, with high levels of violence-related trauma.

### 3.7 How a turno works (composite)

[K/E, a typical large public UEH; private EDs are smaller, calmer and more hierarchical toward specialists "de llamado" (on call from home)]
- **Shift patterns**: doctors on **24-hour shifts** (Ley 15.076) or 12-hour **"largo"** (day) and **"noche"** (night) shifts; nurses and TENS on the **"cuarto turno"** (long–night–off–off). Many doctors **"doblan"** (double) or moonlight in private EDs after a public shift.
- **08:00 entrega de turno** (handover): bed-by-bed, stretcher-by-stretcher, including the patients "hospitalized in the ED" since yesterday. Tempers are short at 08:00.
- **Flow**: admisión (registration, insurance check) → **categorización** (triage by an EU or trained TENS, C1–C5) → box (cubicle) or the **sala de reanimación** → labs, imaging → **"pedir cama"** (request a bed) → wait, often in the **pasillo** (corridor) → transfer to the ward, ICU or another hospital, or discharge with an **interconsulta** (referral).
- **Paperwork**: the **DAU (Dato de Atención de Urgencia)**; prescriptions; **constatación de lesiones** for assaults and domestic violence (forms for police and prosecutors); **alcoholemia** blood samples after traffic crashes (central after Ley 20.580 "Tolerancia Cero", 2012, and Ley 20.770 "Ley Emilia", 2014) [K]; Ley de Urgencia certificates and notifications in private EDs (file 04); death certificates; GES notification forms.
- **Night**: the drunk, the stabbed, the elderly with pneumonia, the overdoses, the psychiatric patient restrained on a stretcher, the 04:00 arrest.

| Level | Medical | Nursing and support |
|---|---|---|
| Top | **Jefe/a de servicio** (head of ED) | **Enfermera supervisora** |
| Shift | **Jefe/a de turno** (often a surgeon in 2005; an urgenciólogo by the 2020s) | EU de turno (charge nurse) |
| Staff | Médicos de turno (GPs, urgenciólogos, surgeons, internists) | EU clínicas |
| Training | **Becados** (residents) | Nursing students |

**Class runs through it** [E]: doctors from private schools and the barrio alto; TENS and auxiliares from the same poblaciones as the patients. Nurses are the true managers of the ED. The *auxiliar* who has worked thirty years at the Posta knows more about where things are than any jefe.

### 3.10 Overcrowding and the winter campaign

- **Pacientes en pasillo / hospitalizados en urgencia**: patients with an admission order who wait in the ED for a ward or ICU bed, sometimes for days. Public hospitals report the share of patients waiting **more than 12 hours** for a bed [K].
- **Campaña de Invierno** [K]: each winter (roughly May–August) respiratory viruses (influenza, RSV, later SARS-CoV-2) swamp paediatric and adult EDs; MINSAL funds extra staff, **reconverts beds** (ward beds to critical care, elective surgery postponed), extends SAPU hours and buys private beds via the UGCC. The 2009 H1N1 season showed the model: beds reconverted to advanced care and a **central coordination station rationing critical beds** [V].
- **Smog**: winter **pre-emergencias and emergencias ambientales** (air-quality alerts) in Santiago coincide with the respiratory surge [K].
- **Low-acuity load**: many patients use EDs for problems primary care could solve (C4–C5), because primary care is closed or waiting times are long [K].

### 3.11 Violence against staff

[K/E] Verbal and physical aggression in EDs is endemic: drunk patients, desperate relatives, gang members. In the **2020s** the press reported shootings near or inside health facilities in southern and western Santiago, armed groups escorting wounded members and intimidating staff, and attacks on ambulance crews. Penal reforms in the late 2010s increased penalties for assaulting health workers [K, law number to verify]. Security guards, metal doors and "botones de pánico" multiplied. A private network in central Santiago will face this from the first month.

### 4.4 Case studies

#### H1N1, 2009 [V]
Between May and September 2009 Chile reported **366,624 cases**, **12,248 confirmed**, **1,562 hospitalized** and **132 deaths** (0.78 per 100,000). **17%** of ICU patients needed high-frequency oscillatory ventilation or **ECMO**. EDs and outpatient services added staff and extended hours; antivirals were free; hospital beds were reconverted to advanced care and a **central coordination station rationed critical beds** (Ugarte et al., *Crit Care Med* 2010). In two Santiago hospitals, ICU admission cost about **US$ 20,300 per patient**.

#### The 27F earthquake and tsunami, 27 February 2010
- **Facts**: Saturday **27 Feb 2010, 03:34**, magnitude **8.8**, off the Maule coast; tsunami on the coast and Juan Fernández; **≈ 525 deaths** [K]. Coastal towns were devastated (80% of Dichato destroyed [V]). Looting in Concepción led to a military curfew [K]. ONEMI and the Navy's oceanographic service were criticized for the failed tsunami warning [K].
- **Health system** [V]: significant damage, including the **complete destruction of 10 hospitals**; field hospitals and foreign medical teams were valued by communities; mental-health response was weak for lack of local plans and drills; the poor were hardest hit (López Tagle et al., *Rev Panam Salud Pública* 2011). Medical students and young doctors formed volunteer brigades to reach isolated coves and villages [V].
- **Santiago**: less damaged than the south but with collapsed older buildings, evacuated hospital wards and damaged hospitals [K, which hospitals to verify], power and phone failures for hours to days, fuel and supply disruption. An EM doctor in Santiago that morning would see crush injuries, falls, panic, cardiac events, and then a flood of transfers from the south.
- **Game note**: it fell **two weeks before a change of government** (Bachelet to Piñera, 11 March 2010) [K].

#### San Miguel prison fire, 8 December 2010
[K] A fire during a fight in a tower of the overcrowded **Centro de Detención Preventiva San Miguel** killed **81 inmates**. Survivors with burns and smoke inhalation went mainly to the HUAP burns unit. It became the emblem of Chile's prison overcrowding. **Game use**: a predawn burns-and-inhalation MCI with prisoners under guard, desperate families at the gates and a press storm.

#### Other events, 2014–2017
- **Great fire of Valparaíso, 12–13 April 2014**: about 15 deaths, thousands of homes on the hills [K].
- **Escuela Militar Metro "subcentro" bomb, 8 September 2014**: an explosive device in a shopping arcade at a Metro station in Las Condes injured about 14 people [K].
- **Illapel earthquake (Mw 8.3), 16 September 2015**, and the **central-south megafires of January–February 2017** [K].

#### The estallido social, October 2019 →
- **Facts**: mass protests from **18 October 2019**; Metro stations burned; state of emergency and curfew for about ten days; protests continued for months around **Plaza Italia / Baquedano**, less than a kilometre from the HUAP [K].
- **Ocular trauma** [V]: the **UTO (Eye Trauma Unit) at Hospital del Salvador**, the national referral centre, saw **259 patients from 18 Oct to 30 Nov 2019**; in **182 (70.5%)** the suspected cause was **kinetic impact projectiles** (riot-control pellets and rounds). These patients were mostly young men (median age 26); **48.9%** had severe visual impairment or blindness at first examination; **20.3%** were open-globe injuries; **13** required evisceration (Rodríguez Á et al., *Eye* 2020/2021; "the largest case series recorded in international literature"). Cumulative national counts reported by human-rights bodies were higher (on the order of 400+ eye injuries) [K].
- **Reparations**: MINSAL created a programme for people with eye and other injuries caused by state agents (an ocular and dento-maxillofacial prosthetic care and rehabilitation programme) [V]. A dental case series from it found injuries mostly from projectiles and physical force [V].
- **Public figures** [K]: student **Gustavo Gatica** (blinded in both eyes, 8 November 2019) and **Fabiola Campillai** (blinded by a tear-gas canister, 26 November 2019; later elected senator).
- **The health side** [K/E]: SAMU calls doubled [R]; volunteer **brigadas de salud** of students, nurses and doctors set up first-aid posts at Plaza Italia; ambulances and clinics were caught in tear gas; health workers documented injuries; tension between hospital staff and police. A stabilization unit near Plaza Italia would be at the centre of everything: politically exposed whatever it does.

#### COVID-19, 2020–2022
- **Facts** [K]: first case **3 March 2020**; dynamic quarantines; the **"Paso a Paso"** plan; peaks of ICU occupancy in **mid-2020** and again in **the first half of 2021**; mass vaccination from early 2021, among the fastest in the world; around 60,000+ deaths (confirmed and probable) by 2022–23.
- **Critical beds** [V/K]: at the start, Chile's critical-care beds (UCI and UTI) were estimated at **1,900–2,000** nationwide (Peña & Espinosa, *Medwave* 2020) [V]. The system expanded to roughly **4,000+ ICU-level beds** at its peak by reconverting operating theatres, recovery rooms, intermediate units and paediatric units [K, exact peak to verify]. Across Ibero-America, ICU capacity rose **69%** during the pandemic, mostly by redeploying non-intensivists rather than training new ones [V].
- **Centralization**: the **UGCC** allocated critical beds across the **public and private** sectors as a single **Red Integrada** [K/R]; Fonasa COVID patients in private clinics were billed under **GRD agreements at public-network (MAI) rates** [R]; patients were moved between regions, including by **FACh aircraft** configured for intensive care [K].
- **A Santiago example** [V]: at **Hospital El Carmen de Maipú**, the six-bed paediatric ICU was handed to adult COVID care for 20 weeks and grew to a **23-bed** COVID unit; staff went from **40 to 125** in 24-hour shifts; 136 patients were admitted, 48% ventilated, with **15%** in-hospital mortality (Díaz et al., *J Crit Care* 2021).
- **Game use**: the government becomes the main buyer of ICU days; oxygen, sedatives (midazolam, fentanyl), propofol and neuromuscular blockers run short; nurses quit; doctors burn out; the network's ICU is commandeered or rented. A prepper Founder with stockpiles faces the choice of selling, donating or hoarding.

#### The Valparaíso-region fires, February 2024
- **Facts** [V]: **2–6 February 2024**, the most devastating **wildland-urban interface fire** in Chile's history, mainly in Viña del Mar (and Quilpué and Villa Alemana); thousands of homes lost and **more than a hundred deaths** (the official toll settled around **137**) [V for >100; K for 137]. Smoke drove **fine-particle (PM2.5) levels 198% above the Chilean standard** for days (*Sci Total Environ* 2024) [V].
- **Medical pattern** [K/E]: most deaths occurred at the scene (fire overtook people on hillside streets); the hospitalized survivors were fewer: burns, inhalation injury, carbon-monoxide poisoning, falls, decompensated chronic disease, and later mental-health consequences. Regional hospitals (Gustavo Fricke in Viña, Carlos Van Buren in Valparaíso) took the first wave; complex burns could be transferred to Santiago's burns centres [K/E].

#### The national blackout, 25 February 2025
[K] On **25 February 2025**, mid-afternoon, a transmission failure blacked out Chile **from Arica to Los Lagos**, about 98% of the population, for several hours (most supply returned overnight). The Metro stopped; traffic lights died; a curfew was declared for the night. **Hospitals ran on generators**; patients on home ventilators and oxygen concentrators sought help; cold chains and dialysis were at risk; phone networks degraded as backup batteries ran down. **Game use**: the first scene of Era II could be this afternoon. Which units' generators start, and which do not?

#### Metro and bus mass-casualty incidents, gas explosions
- **Not verified**: a gas explosion in the **Bellas Artes** area of Santiago Centro around 2019, as suggested in the brief, could not be confirmed during this research. Treat specific dates as unverified.
- Plausible event types for play [E]: **LPG cylinder ("balón de gas") explosions** in homes and small restaurants; natural-gas leaks in old buildings of Santiago Centro; industrial fires and chemical leaks in western industrial comunas; bus crashes on mountain and interurban roads (the Ruta 68, the cuestas) whose victims reach Santiago; Metro platform incidents (falls and suicides, crowd crush on protest days); the 2019 Metro arsons (fires without mass casualties) [K].

### 4.5 Who trained in disaster medicine

[K/E unless marked]
- **ATLS** (American College of Surgeons) has been taught in Chile since the 1990s through the Chilean chapter; by 2005 it was common for trauma surgeons and some ED doctors [K].
- **PHTLS** (pre-hospital trauma) and **ACLS/PALS** (American Heart Association) taught by local training centres; SAMU and Bomberos trained their crews in pre-hospital trauma [K].
- **BDLS/ADLS** (US National Disaster Life Support) and **MIMMS/HMIMMS** (UK major-incident courses): **availability in Chile during the period could not be verified** [gap].
- University **diplomados** in emergency and disaster management have existed at several universities (Universidad de Chile, PUC and others), often aimed at nurses and managers; exact names and dates **unverified** [gap].
- In 2005, a Chilean doctor seriously interested in disaster medicine would have built a **patchwork**: ATLS and ACLS (instructor level), SAMU work, armed-forces or Bomberos contacts, and **courses abroad** (France via the SAMU link, Spain, the USA, Israel) [E]. After 27F the field grew quickly: hospital emergency plans, drills, MINSAL disaster units, CIGIDEN research [K/E].

## 6. Hooks for play (fiction ideas)

*All of the following are fiction prompts built on the realities above, not events that happened.*

1. **The cheque at the desk (2005).** A new receptionist at the flagship asks the wife of a man with a dissecting aorta for "un cheque en garantía". She complies, and he survives. Weeks later she files a complaint with the brand-new Superintendencia, and an inspector arrives with a copy of Ley 19.650.
2. **Who certifies stability?** A Fonasa patient after a damage-control laparotomy. Management needs the bed; the UGCC has offered a bed at Barros Luco in four hours. The young doctor on shift refuses to sign the stabilization certificate. The family is being asked to sign a DOMA for "libre elección".
3. **The rebase economy (2016).** The network's four ICU beds have been full of Fonasa patients waiting for public beds for three weeks, paid by Fonasa. Finance loves it. Then a polytrauma arrives by ambulance and there is nowhere to put him.
4. **The Posta's favour.** A HUAP jefe de turno who moonlights for the Founder offers to "find a bed" for his transfers in exchange for shifts, cash or a job for his nephew. *Pituto* or corruption?
5. **The mutual's audit.** ACHS reviews 50 work-accident bills and claims two thirds were overbilled. A senior nurse knows who altered the codes.
6. **27F at 03:34.** The flagship's old building cracks; the generator starts on the second try; the phones die; the first car arrives with a family crushed in an adobe house in the south of Santiago. And the transfers from Talca begin at dawn.
7. **San Miguel, 05:30 (8 Dec 2010).** The unit nearest the prison receives three burned inmates and a gendarme. Gendarmería wants them back in custody; the HUAP's burns unit is full.
8. **The accreditation deadline (July 2016).** Without accreditation, the Isapre GES contract for polytrauma ends. The quality nurse finds that the adverse-event log has been "cleaned".
9. **Plaza Italia, 18 October 2019 onward.** The unit is 600 metres from the protests. Students with ocular injuries arrive; carabineros arrive with a detainee and demand the medical report; volunteer brigades ask for supplies; a TV crew films the entrance. Every choice is political.
10. **Midazolam and oxygen (June 2020).** The UGCC needs ICU beds; the network has a prepper's stockpile of sedatives, oxygen concentrators and two spare ventilators in a basement. Sell to the State, donate, or keep for the end?
11. **The Isapre that doesn't pay (2023).** An Isapre in crisis delays payment for nine months on 300 million pesos of Ley de Urgencia bills. Payroll is in eleven days.
12. **Balas locas (2022).** A child hit by a stray bullet in La Pintana; the gang that fired returns to the unit to look for the rival they wounded. The guard has a baton.
13. **Viña burns (February 2024).** A request from the Servicio de Salud: send two advanced ambulances and a burns-capable team to the Fricke. That leaves Santiago's south uncovered for 48 hours.
14. **The blackout (25 February 2025).** The Heir's first week. 15:16: all lights go out. One unit's generator fails to start; a ventilated patient is on hand-bagging; the phones are failing; the bunker beneath the flagship has its own power. Who knows that?
15. **The specialist who was never certified.** A beloved veteran *médico de urgencia* has worked at the network for fifteen years. A Superintendencia registry check shows he never passed CONACEM, and a malpractice mediation for a missed epidural haematoma names him.
