## 1. Structure of the system

Chile has a **dual (public/private) insurance system** funded by a mandatory **7% health contribution** (cotización de salud) on taxable income, up to a cap (tope imponible, quoted in UF) [K]. Each worker chooses to send the 7% either to the public insurer **Fonasa** or to a private **Isapre**. Separately, **employers** pay for workplace accidents through the **mutuales** (Ley 16.744). Armed forces and police have their **own systems**. Providers (hospitals, clinics) are public or private, and every insurer can buy from either side.

### 1.1 Who is insured where (approximate)

| Insurer | Share of population 2005 | Share ≈ 2025 | Who | Main providers used |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fonasa** (Fondo Nacional de Salud, public, created 1979) | ≈ 68–72% | ≈ 78–82% | Low- and middle-income workers, dependants, pensioners, indigents, most migrants | Public network (MAI); private providers with Fonasa agreement (MLE, tramos B–D) |
| **Isapres** (Instituciones de Salud Previsional, private, from 1981) | ≈ 16–18% | ≈ 14–15% (after the 2022–24 exodus) | Higher earners, younger and healthier on average | Private clinics; Isapre "preferred provider" networks; public pensionado beds |
| **FF.AA. / police** (Capredena, Dipreca) | ≈ 2–3% | ≈ 2–3% | Military, Carabineros, PDI, Gendarmería, families | Hospital Militar, Hospital FACh, Hospital Naval (Viña), Hospital Dipreca, Hospital de Carabineros |
| None / private pay ("particular") | ≈ 3–5% | ≈ 2–4% | Some independents, wealthy, irregular cases | Pay out of pocket |

All shares **[K/E]**: order of magnitude is reliable, exact figures vary by source and year.

### 1.2 Fonasa: tramos, MAI and MLE

**Tramos (income bands)** [K]:

| Tramo | Who | Public network (MAI) copay | Private (MLE)? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | No income: indigent, beneficiaries of non-contributory pensions, (since ~2016) undocumented migrants who register | 0% | **No**: MAI only |
| **B** | Taxable income ≤ minimum wage | 0% | Yes |
| **C** | Income > minimum wage and ≤ 1.46 × minimum wage (and ≥3 dependants moves you down to B) | 10% until Sept 2022, then 0% | Yes |
| **D** | Income > 1.46 × minimum wage | 20% until Sept 2022, then 0% | Yes |

- **MAI, Modalidad de Atención Institucional**: care in the public network (CESFAM, SAPU/SAR, public hospitals). Since **September 2022 "Copago Cero"** removed copays for all tramos in MAI, including care given under the Ley de Urgencia **in the public network** [V]. Copago Cero does **not** apply in private clinics, nor to MLE bonos, PAD packages or pensionado beds [V].
- **MLE, Modalidad de Libre Elección**: tramos B–D buy a **bono** (voucher) or **programa médico** to see private providers registered with Fonasa at **level 1, 2 or 3** (higher level, higher price, same Fonasa contribution, so a higher copay) [V].
- **PAD (Pago Asociado a Diagnóstico)**: fixed-price surgical packages in MLE (cholecystectomy, hernia, delivery…) [V].
- **GRD (Grupos Relacionados por el Diagnóstico)**: DRG-style per-discharge payment Fonasa uses with public hospitals and, under contract, with private clinics (prominent in COVID-19 and in 2020s tenders to clear waiting lists) [R].
- **Modalidad de Cobertura Complementaria (MCC)**: a supplementary-insurance option for Fonasa B–D created in the 2024 Isapre "ley corta" package and rolled out in 2025 with a contracted insurer and closed provider network [K, details to verify]. In Era II it is a new buyer the enterprise could court.

### 1.3 Isapres

- **Open (abiertas)**: anyone can join: Banmédica, Vida Tres, Colmena, Consalud, Cruz Blanca (Bupa), Nueva Masvida, Esencial (2020s) and others [K]. **Closed (cerradas)**: only for workers of a company or sector (historically Codelco divisions, banks, etc.) [K].
- The member signs a **plan** (thousands of them) with a price in UF; it sets hospital and outpatient coverage % and caps, often with a **preferred network** (plan cerrado/preferente). On top of the plan, by law: **GES** coverage (fixed GES premium, §3) and **CAEC** (Cobertura Adicional para Enfermedades Catastróficas, since 2000; catastrophic cover after a deductible of roughly 30 × the monthly contribution, with a minimum and maximum in UF, used only in the Isapre's CAEC network) [K].
- **Ownership** [V for Banmédica]: Banmédica (Isapres Banmédica and Vida Tres, Clínicas Santa María and Dávila, and the rescue firm **Help**) was controlled by Empresas Penta until UnitedHealth Group bought it in 2018. UnitedHealth announced in 2024 that it would sell its South American business, and a sale to an entity linked to the private-equity firm Patria was later reported [V/R]. Bupa bought Cruz Blanca and Integramédica in the mid-2010s [K].
- **Crisis 2022–2024** [K]: after a 2010 Constitutional Court ruling against the age/sex "tabla de factores" and years of court appeals ("recursos de protección") against annual price hikes, the **Supreme Court (late 2022)** ordered Isapres to apply a single factor table and refund overcharges. The "ley corta de Isapres" (2024, **Ley 21.674** [K, verify number]) set a repayment scheme over many years. Hundreds of thousands of members moved to Fonasa; Isapres posted heavy losses; clinics feared unpaid bills. **Game effect**: from 2022 the enterprise's best-paying payer is wobbling; slower payments, tougher audits of bills, more Fonasa patients in private EDs.
- **Isapre Masvida** collapsed in 2016–2017 (members transferred, providers left with unpaid bills) [K]. Good model for a "payer goes bust" event.

### 1.4 Workplace accidents: the mutuales and Ley 16.744 (1968)

- **Ley 16.744** (1968) created compulsory insurance for **accidentes del trabajo y enfermedades profesionales**, paid entirely by employers: a basic contribution (≈0.90% of payroll) plus a risk-differentiated surcharge (0–3.4%) [K]. It covers 100% of care, rehabilitation, sick pay and pensions. It also covers **accidentes de trayecto** (commuting accidents) [K].
- Administrators [K]:

| Administrator | Founded | Flagship facility in Santiago | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ACHS** (Asociación Chilena de Seguridad) | 1958 | **Hospital del Trabajador** (Providencia) | Largest mutual; HT is a major trauma, burns, spine and hand-surgery centre |
| **Mutual de Seguridad CChC** (Cámara Chilena de la Construcción) | 1966 | Hospital Clínico Mutual de Seguridad (Alameda, Estación Central area) | Strong in construction trauma |
| **IST** (Instituto de Seguridad del Trabajo) | 1957 | Head office in Viña del Mar; clinics in Santiago | Smallest of the three |
| **ISL** (Instituto de Seguridad Laboral) | 2009 (successor of the INP's accident role) | Uses the public network | Public; covers employers not in a mutual |

- **Seguro escolar** (school accident insurance, Ley 16.744 art. 3 and DS 313/1972): pupils' and students' accidents are covered free, **in the public network** [K].
- **Game relevance**: a worker crushed on a building site in Santiago Centro is a **mutual patient**. If the enterprise stabilizes him, it bills the mutual, which then wants him moved to its own hospital fast. A **contract (convenio) with a mutual** for first response and stabilization in a district is a realistic, steady revenue line. Mutuales pay reliably but audit hard [E].

### 1.5 SOAP: traffic accidents

- **SOAP** (Seguro Obligatorio de Accidentes Personales, Ley 18.490, 1986): every motor vehicle must carry it. It pays **medical and hospital expenses up to 300 UF per victim**, plus death and disability benefits [K]. That is ≈ CLP 5.2 million in 2005 and ≈ CLP 11.5 million in 2025, enough to pay for most of a stabilization.
- The provider bills the SOAP insurer (with the Carabineros report) first; the patient's health insurer pays the remainder [K]. **Traffic trauma therefore pays well for the first 300 UF**, which suits a stabilize-and-transfer business.

### 1.6 Armed forces and police

- **Capredena** (Caja de Previsión de la Defensa Nacional) for Army, Navy, Air Force; **Dipreca** (Dirección de Previsión de Carabineros) for Carabineros, PDI and Gendarmería [K]. They have their own hospitals (Hospital Militar de Santiago in La Reina, rebuilt 2008 with seismic isolation; Hospital Clínico FACh; Hospital Dipreca; Hospital de Carabineros; Hospital Naval Almirante Nef in Viña del Mar) and buy from private clinics [K].
- A wounded carabinero is usually moved to the Hospital de Carabineros/Dipreca as soon as he is stable: another "transfer target" with its own politics [E].

### 1.7 Providers in Santiago

**Public network**: since the 1980 reform the Región Metropolitana (RM) is divided into **six Servicios de Salud** (territorial health services that run the public hospitals) [K]. Primary care (CESFAM) is run by the **municipalities**. Out-of-hours primary urgent care: **SAPU** (Servicio de Atención Primaria de Urgencia, from 1990) and, from ~2015, the larger **SAR** (Servicio de Alta Resolutividad, with X-ray and basic labs) [K].

## 2. The Ley de Urgencia: exactly how it works

> This is the most important legal mechanism for the enterprise. The business model ("get the patient, stabilize, transfer") is exactly the window the law covers.

### 2.1 The legal sources (timeline)

### 2.2 What counts as an emergency, and who decides

- **Definition**: a condition with **risk to life or of serious functional sequelae** requiring immediate, non-deferrable care [V]. A 2011 review by SAMU physicians (Tay & Becerra, *Rev Méd Clín Las Condes*) frames it as a threat to life **or** risk of losing an organ or limb, needing immediate care [V].
- **Who certifies**: a **médico cirujano of the emergency unit** (public or private) where the patient is seen [V]. In practice: the ED doctor writes the certificate (certificado de urgencia vital / de emergencia). The certification is clinical, but the **insurer audits it afterwards**: Fonasa or the Isapre may reject it as "not a vital emergency", and that is the origin of most disputes (§2.8).
- **After DS 34 (Oct 2022)** the certification must fit the regulation's **general clinical conditions** (art. 23 for adults; the detailed parameters are set by ministerial resolution). In game terms, from 2022 the paperwork is stricter: the certificate must name which condition applies, and auditors check it [V for structure; detailed list not retrieved].
- **Stabilization**: the moment when the patient's **vital functions are in equilibrium, or the risk of serious functional sequelae has been overcome**, *even if the underlying disease is not resolved* [V/R]. It is certified by the **treating physician** (médico cirujano of the unit where the patient is). Stabilization ends the Ley de Urgencia period. **The patient can be transferred only after the stabilization is certified** [V].
- Pre-hospital: the ambulance crew does not certify. The protection starts when the patient is received by an emergency unit that certifies [K/E].

### 2.3 The prohibition of guarantees

- In a certified emergency, **no provider (public or private) may demand money, cheques, pagarés, credit-card vouchers, letras de cambio, employer letters or any other financial instrument**, nor otherwise **condition** care on payment [V].
- **Ley 20.394 (2009)** goes further: **cheques and cash can never be demanded as a guarantee for any care**. For **elective** care the provider may still ask for other guarantees (a pagaré, a card authorization, an employer letter) [V].
- **Sanctions** (Superintendencia de Salud, Intendencia de Prestadores): **fines of 10 to 1,000 UTM**, **suspension for up to 180 days** from providing GES and Fonasa-MLE services, and **2 to 4 times the fine** for repeat offences within 12 months [V, as reported c. 2011; check current text]. Procedure set by Circular IP N° 6 (Dec 2009) [V]. A Santiago Court of Appeals ruling has confirmed fines on a clinic for demanding a pagaré in an emergency (art. 141) [V].
- **Patient's practical route**: complain to the Superintendencia; the provider must return the instrument [R].
- **Game texture**: in 2005 it was still common folklore that private clinics "pedían cheque en garantía" at the front desk. Ley 19.650 already banned it for vital emergencies, but the reach of "vital emergency" was disputed and the cheque survived at admissions until 2009 [R: the legislative history of Ley 20.394 says the 1999 ban was limited to physician-certified emergencies, which motivated the 2009 law]. A 2005 receptionist who asks a bleeding man's wife for a cheque is plausible, and illegal.

### 2.4 Who pays, and when: the money flow

**Principle**: during the emergency, **the insurer pays the provider directly** for the services given up to stabilization; the part the patient would normally owe (copay) is **lent** to the patient by the insurer [V].

**Fonasa patients in a private clinic**:
1. The clinic attends and certifies the emergency. It must not ask for guarantees.
2. The patient or a representative must **notify Fonasa** by a verifiable means; the clinic notifies the **UGCC** through the online platform once the patient is stabilized (and in practice early, to start the bed search) [V/R].
3. **Fonasa values the stabilization phase through the MLE arancel, Grupo 28: "Pago Asociado a Emergencia" (PAE)**, a set of emergency payment packages defined in the arancel's Normas Técnico Administrativas [V]. (The 2026 arancel lists 11 PAE items in Group 28 [R]; the values depend on the provider's MLE level.)
4. Fonasa pays the clinic, then **bills the patient his copay by certified letter**. If he does not pay, Fonasa may issue an **automatic loan** for that amount [V]. One secondary (commercial) guide puts the average copay for tramos B–D at **≈30% of the stabilization bill** [R, weak].
5. **What the clinic charges above Fonasa's valuation**: historically a grey zone and a source of complaints (patients receiving large bills). Treat it as disputed: in the game, clinics try to bill the difference, Fonasa and the Superintendencia push back [E; not resolved by sources found].

**Isapre patients**:
1. Same clinical certification; no guarantees.
2. The **Isapre pays the clinic directly** for the services of the emergency period, applying the plan's coverage [V/R].
3. The patient's share becomes a **loan** if he has not paid it to the Isapre within **30 working days after the Isapre paid the provider** [V].
4. **Loan terms** [V]: monthly installments **no larger than 5% of taxable income** (for voluntary members, an amount equal to the plan price); the Isapre **may not demand cheques** to secure the loan; interest at the **"tasa de interés corriente para operaciones reajustables"** (Ley 18.010, art. 6); employees and pensioners repay through the employer or pension payer, independents directly.
5. After stabilization: transfer to the Isapre's network (GES/CAEC network, §3) or stay under ordinary plan coverage.

**Loan terms in general** (Fonasa and Isapre) [R]: installments ≤5% of income for Ley de Urgencia (versus ≤10% for ordinary Fonasa medical loans); maximum term reported as **five years**; the first installment due about 30 days after activation. Sources **disagree on interest**: Fonasa's own pages say "sin interés", while other official summaries say installments are CPI-indexed with ordinary interest. **The debt is inheritable** (Fonasa leaflet, 2026) [R].

| Question | Fonasa | Isapre |
|---|---|---|
| Who pays the clinic during the emergency? | Fonasa (PAE / MLE valuation) | The Isapre |
| Can the clinic ask for a guarantee? | No | No |
| How does the patient repay? | Copay billed by Fonasa; automatic loan if unpaid | Loan if unpaid after 30 working days |
| Installment cap | 5% of income | 5% of income (or plan price for voluntaries) |
| After stabilization | **DOMA**: MAI (transfer, UGCC) or MLE (stay and pay) | Transfer to network (GES/CAEC) or stay under plan |
| If no public bed is available | Fonasa pays the **rebase** (since 2015) | Isapre plan / CAEC rules |

### 2.5 Stabilization, the DOMA, transfer and "rescate"

- **DOMA (Documento de Opción de Modalidad de Atención)**, regulated by DS 34/2022 [V]: once the **certificate of stabilization** is issued, a Fonasa patient (or his representative) signs a choice:
  - **MAI (institutional)**: he will continue in the **public network**. The clinic notifies the UGCC; the public hospital assigned coordinates the transfer; **he stays in the clinic until transferred or discharged** [R].
  - **MLE (free choice)**: he **stays** in the clinic and **assumes the costs from stabilization to discharge** under his MLE coverage [R]. This is where families sign away their savings; tramo A cannot choose MLE [K].
- **UGCC** (Unidad de Gestión Centralizada de Camas, MINSAL Subsecretaría de Redes, operating **since May 2009**) [R]: monitors hospital EDs (UEH) and critical beds daily, manages Ley de Urgencia patients and their "rescate" with the Servicios de Salud, and **buys private beds** under agreements, with a ranking of private providers [R]. Its functions were updated by **Res. Exenta 868 (25 Nov 2021)** [R]. Since **2010** its transfer platform has been software run by **Openagora SpA**; a Contraloría audit criticised that the system logs actions as a "bitácora" (log) without verifiable evidence of decisions [R].
- **Rescate** (retrieval): the public network's duty to come and take the stabilized patient. DS 34 created it as a formal figure [V]. In practice it depends on a public bed existing.
- **Isapre side**: for GES or CAEC patients admitted outside the network, the insurer must be **notified within 24 hours** (the Superintendencia has fined a clinic for missing it); for CAEC the Isapre has **2 working days** from the request to assign a network bed; the treating doctor authorizes the transfer [R].

### 2.6 The rebase, and where the money actually went

- **Rebase** = the post-stabilization stay of a Fonasa patient in a private clinic **because the public network has no bed**. Since **2015** a MINSAL resolution makes **Fonasa pay it**, until transfer or discharge; unlike the emergency phase, **the rebase does not generate a debt for the patient** [R].
- Figures (Fonasa data via the press) [R]:
  - Rebase spending rose from **CLP 10,535 million (2013)** to **CLP 48,984 million (2017)**, a 364% increase in four years. In 2017 Fonasa paid **CLP 30,262 million** for the emergencies themselves and **≈ CLP 49,000 million** for rebase.
  - Only **≈14%** of seriously ill Fonasa patients reaching private clinics were transferred to public hospitals after stabilization (2018 reporting); the average stay in the private clinic was **≈7 days**.
  - **2015–2025**: more than half of Fonasa's Ley de Urgencia spending went on avoidable hospitalizations in private clinics, **≈ CLP 826,000 million** (826 mil millones) in total; effective transfers averaged **≈11%** over the decade (Subsecretaría de Redes data, BioBioChile, Jan 2026).
  - In 2023 Fonasa's budget for private purchases expanded by **48%**, partly to "recognize the real spending" on post-stabilization Ley de Urgencia payments [R].

**What this means**: for private clinics, Ley de Urgencia was rarely a loss-maker in aggregate. The **stabilization package (PAE)** was the part clinics complained about (fixed, below list prices), but the **rebase days**, paid by Fonasa because the public network could not take the patient, became a large and fairly reliable revenue stream after 2015 [R + E]. Before 2015 those days were contested: billed to the patient (who often could not pay), to Fonasa (which often refused), or written off [E].

### 2.7 The disputes (how it goes wrong)

1. **"No era urgencia vital"**: insurer auditors reject the certificate after the fact; the patient is suddenly billed as a private patient. Courts have repeatedly sided with patients in clear cases [V: 2025 Supreme Court ruling; R: other cases].
2. **Over-certification**: clinics (or a tired ED doctor) certify borderline cases to keep the payment flowing. After DS 34 this became riskier [E].
3. **Stabilization timing**: the clinic is accused of certifying stabilization **late** (to extend the covered period) or **early** (to push a Fonasa patient into a DOMA for MLE). Families accuse clinics of pressuring them to sign the DOMA "por la libre elección" [E, plausible; common complaint pattern].
4. **Guarantees in disguise**: pagarés signed "for the post-stabilization part", card pre-authorizations at admission. Legal after stabilization for uncovered services (2022 SC), illegal before [V].
5. **Transfer failures**: the UGCC finds no bed for days; the Servicio de Salud's "rescate" ambulance never comes; the patient deteriorates; who is liable? [R for transfer failures].
6. **Payment lag**: Fonasa and the Isapres pay months later; providers carry receivables; in the 2022–24 crisis clinics feared Isapre insolvency [K/E].
7. **Debt collection**: patients chased for copays; credit-bureau reporting of medical debts restricted since the 2012 "Ley DICOM" reform (the Superintendencia polices clinics on both the cheque law and "Ley DICOM") [R; exact scope not verified].

### 2.8 Applying it to a "stabilize and transfer" business

**The core fit.** The enterprise operates exactly inside the Ley de Urgencia window: it receives the critical patient, certifies the emergency, does damage control, certifies stabilization, and transfers. The law guarantees that **someone pays** for that window (Fonasa, an Isapre, a mutual, SOAP), and forbids the business from demanding money first.

**Rules of thumb for the DM**:

| Situation | 2005–2008 | 2009–2014 | 2015–2022 | 2022–2025+ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Can the unit refuse a critical walk-in with no money? | No (vital emergency, Ley 19.650) | No; and no cheque for anything (Ley 20.394) | No | No |
| Who funds the stabilization of a Fonasa patient? | Fonasa via MLE emergency packages; patient copay via loan | Same | Same (PAE) | Same, but stricter certification (DS 34) |
| Fonasa patient stabilized, no public bed | Disputed: unit carries cost, bills patient or argues with Fonasa | UGCC exists (from 2009), still disputed | **Fonasa pays rebase** (profitable occupied bed) | Same; Contraloría and press scrutiny of rebase |
| Isapre patient | Best payer: full plan coverage, Isapre lends copay | Same | Same | Payer risk during Isapre crisis |
| Work accident | Bill the mutual; transfer to HT/Mutual quickly | Same | Same | Same |
| Traffic accident | SOAP first 300 UF, then insurer | Same | Same | Same |

**Structural problems a stabilization network faces** [E, reasoned from the law and the data above]:
- **"Stabilized" is not "treated"**: a damage-control laparotomy patient is not stable until after surgery and some hours to days of ICU. The unit therefore needs **ICU beds and the staff to hold patients for 1–7 days**. If it has no beds, it cannot legally transfer an unstable patient, and it gets stuck.
- **Transfers depend on others' beds**: the public network's transfer rate after private stabilization ran at only ~11–14%. A business plan that assumes "transfer within 6 hours" will fail in winter. The realistic plan is to **own the downstream**: the central clinic takes the Isapre and MLE patients; agreements with the mutuales and the military hospitals; and the UGCC's bed-purchase agreements (which turn the unit's spare ICU beds into a revenue line instead of a cost).
- **Rebase as a business**: after 2015, a Fonasa patient waiting for a public bed in the unit's ICU is paid for by Fonasa. That is good revenue but **blocks the bed** that the next trauma needs, and attracts accusations of "living off the rebase".
- **Certification integrity**: the unit's doctors certify both the emergency and the stabilization. An auditor or journalist who finds a pattern of convenient certifications is a scandal hook. So is a doctor who refuses to certify stabilization under pressure from management.
- **No guarantees at the door**: front-desk staff training, signage, and the temptation to "ask for a card just in case". From November 2009 one complaint to the Superintendencia can bring a fine of up to 1,000 UTM (≈ CLP 37 million in 2009, ≈ CLP 67 million in 2025) and a suspension from GES/MLE [V for range]. Before Ley 20.394 the sanction regime was weaker and less enforced [K, details unverified], so in 2005–2009 the main risk is a complaint, a court case and the press.
- **Paperwork is a weapon**: notification within 24 h to the insurer (GES/CAEC), UGCC platform entries, DOMA signed by the right person, certificates timed correctly. Missing any one of them shifts the bill to the business or to the patient.

## 3. GES / AUGE (Ley 19.966)

### 3.1 What it is

- **Ley 19.966** (published 2004) created the **Régimen de Garantías Explícitas en Salud** (GES, originally "Plan AUGE"). It **came into force on 1 July 2005** with **25 health problems** [K, high confidence]. The list then expanded by decree: **40 (July 2006), 56 (July 2007), 69 (2010), 80 (2013), 85 (2019)** and later additions to **≈90 by 2025** [K for the sequence; the latest counts should be checked].
- Four **explicit guarantees** for every listed condition [K]:
  1. **Acceso** (access): Fonasa and Isapres must cover it.
  2. **Oportunidad** (timeliness): maximum waiting times for diagnosis, treatment and follow-up.
  3. **Protección financiera**: copay capped at **20% of the GES tariff** (0% for Fonasa A and B; and since 2022, 0% in the public network for all), with an annual cap on GES spending per family.
  4. **Calidad**: care by a **registered** professional (Superintendencia registry) and, for institutional providers, by **accredited** establishments once the quality guarantee is in force (for high-complexity closed care: **1 July 2016**, §4.6) [V for 2016].
- GES care must be delivered **in the insurer's GES network** (public network for Fonasa; contracted providers for each Isapre). **Exception: a vital emergency** can be treated anywhere; once stabilized, the patient is transferred into the network, and the provider must notify (within 24 h, §2.5) [R].

### 3.2 GES conditions that are emergencies

| GES problem | Year added (approx.) | Emergency guarantee in practice | Relevance to a stabilization network |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Infarto agudo del miocardio** (STEMI/AMI) | **2005** (first 25) [K] | Very short time limits for ECG diagnosis and **thrombolysis** where indicated (on the order of 30 minutes) [K, verify exact limits] | A unit able to thrombolyse or transfer to a cath lab fast is valuable; the 30-minute clock is a scene generator |
| **Accidente cerebrovascular isquémico** (ischemic stroke, ≥15 y) | 2006 [K] | Diagnosis (CT) and admission within short limits; thrombolysis added later in practice [K] | CT on site is decisive |
| **Politraumatizado grave** (severe polytrauma) | 2007 [K] | Immediate care and resolution at a trauma-capable centre [K] | The core business |
| **TEC moderado o grave** (moderate/severe traumatic brain injury) | 2007 [K] | Immediate assessment, CT and neurosurgical access [K] | Needs neurosurgery downstream (Asenjo, HUAP, private) |
| **Gran quemado** (major burns) | 2007 [K] | Immediate care, transfer to burns centre [K] | Adult reference: HUAP burns unit; also Hospital del Trabajador |
| **Trauma ocular grave** (severe eye trauma) | 2007 [K] | Specialist care within short limits [K] | Reference: UTO, Hospital del Salvador [V] |
| **Hemorragia subaracnoidea por rotura de aneurisma** | 2007 [K] | Diagnosis and neurosurgical/endovascular care [K] | Transfer target |
| **Urgencia odontológica ambulatoria** | 2007 [K] | Ambulatory | Low value |
| Pneumonia ≥65 (ambulatory), IRA <5 y (ambulatory), asthma, COPD exacerbations | 2005–2006 [K] | Ambulatory/medical | Winter volume |
| Respiratory distress of the newborn, prematurity | 2005–2006 [K] | Neonatal | Not the unit's business |

**What GES means for the network** [E]:
- **Opportunity**: a GES patient's insurer must deliver within guaranteed times. Isapres that lack trauma capacity in a zone may **contract the enterprise as part of their GES network** for polytrauma, TBI or AMI stabilization. That is a legitimate, stable revenue line, but after **July 2016** it requires **accreditation** (for high-complexity closed care).
- **Constraint**: a GES patient treated outside the network as a vital emergency must be **notified** and **moved to the network** once stable. Mistakes cost the patient his GES protection and the business its payment.
- **Clock pressure**: GES timeliness creates paper trails. If the unit delays a STEMI transfer and the guarantee is breached, the insurer is liable and will look for whom to blame.

### 4.2 The operating permit (autorización sanitaria)

- Legal base: **Código Sanitario** (DFL 725/1967): health establishments need the sanitary authority's authorization [K]. For inpatient establishments: **DS 161/1982, Reglamento de Hospitales y Clínicas** [V]: defines hospital (organized in differentiated clinical services and support units) and clinic (inpatient care without those differentiated services) [V]; requires an independent and adequate building; the application includes location, owner, title deeds, **permanent supply of sterile material and instruments, emergency drugs**, and a **fire/catastrophe evacuation plan** [V]; at least one health professional on continuous night duty (art. 26) [V]; a **director técnico** (medical director), whose functions were updated by **DS 35/2020** [V].
- **DS 58/2008** "normas técnicas básicas" for the authorization of health establishments (minimum structure, equipment and staffing per unit type) [K, verify number/year].
- Outpatient procedure rooms and minor-surgery theatres: **DS 283/1997** [K].
- **Ambulatory-only "stabilization units"**: a resus room with observation but **no inpatient beds** might be authorized as an outpatient emergency service; once it has an **ICU or overnight beds** it falls under DS 161 as a clinic [E: the classification question is exactly the kind of grey zone a SEREMI inspector would raise].
- **Timeline and cost** [E]: the SEREMI fee itself is modest (hundreds of thousands to low millions of CLP); the real cost is **compliance**: architecture (circulation, isolation, minimum areas), HVAC for theatres, medical gases, electrical backup, fire safety, sterilization, waste, staffing plan. Realistic time from first plans to authorization for a new small clinic: **9–18 months in 2005, 12–24 months in 2025** (more paperwork, accreditation preparation, municipal permits). Converting an existing building in Santiago Centro is quicker but risks structural or seismic problems.

### 4.5 The Superintendencia and accreditation

- **Accreditation** = periodic evaluation of an authorized provider against a MINSAL standard, carried out by private **entidades acreditadoras** authorized and supervised by the Superintendencia; the provider pays the fee; valid for **3 years** [V/K].
- Legal basis: Ley 19.937; **DS 15/2007** (Reglamento del Sistema de Acreditación); the general standards for closed and open care (c. 2009) [R].
- First accreditations of clinics and hospitals around **2009–2010** [K].
- **1 July 2016**: the GES **quality guarantee** came into force for **high-complexity closed-care providers**: they **must be accredited** to deliver GES [V]. 104 establishments were in scope (63 public) [V]. On the day: **48 of 62 public** providers and **36 of 57 private clinics** were accredited [V/R]. By September 2017: **99% of public hospitals and 97% of private high-complexity clinics** [V]. Later stages for medium/low complexity, imaging, laboratories, dialysis and others were rolled out from 2019 with delays [R].
- About **870 accredited providers** nationally by February 2025 [R].
- **Game effect**: from 2016 an unaccredited unit **cannot be part of an Isapre's GES network** for polytrauma/TBI/AMI, which is a major revenue door closing. Accreditation consumes 12–24 months of preparation (protocols, adverse-event reporting, staff credentials, audits) [E].

### 5.7 A small unit's economics (sketch)

**Hypothetical stabilization unit, 2025**: 2 resus bays, 1 emergency OR (on call), 4 ICU beds, 6 observation chairs, 2 advanced ambulances [E]:
- Staff ≈ 70–100 FTE; payroll **CLP 250–400 M/month**; other running costs CLP 80–150 M/month. Break-even needs **CLP 350–550 M/month** of collected revenue.
- That is roughly: **8–15 serious stabilizations a day** at blended CLP 0.8–1.5 M, plus ICU days at 85% occupancy, plus contracts. Isapre-heavy mix: comfortable. Fonasa-heavy mix with no rebase and no contracts: loses money.
- **2005 equivalent**: payroll CLP 70–120 M/month; break-even ≈ CLP 100–170 M/month.
- **Cash-flow trap**: insurers pay 60–180 days late; a growing network can be profitable and still run out of cash [E].

## 6. The professional landscape

### 6.1 Organizations and certification

- **Colegio Médico de Chile** (Colmed, founded 1948): the medical association. Membership has been **voluntary since 1981** [K]; it keeps regional councils, ethics tribunals and a strong political voice; its legal-aid foundation **FALMED** defends members in malpractice cases [K]. Presidents who were national public figures include **Enrique Paris** (2011–2017, later Minister of Health) and **Izkia Siches** (2017–2022, the first woman to lead it, later Minister of the Interior) [K].
- **CONACEM** (Corporación Nacional Autónoma de Certificación de Especialidades Médicas, 1984): certifies specialists, including those trained "in service" without a formal residency, by exam and portfolio [K]. Since the 2007 specialty-certification regulation (and its 2013 successor) MINSAL recognizes a list of specialties, including **Medicina de Urgencia**; the Superintendencia keeps the **Registro Nacional de Prestadores Individuales de Salud** (public, searchable, operating from about 2009) [K].
- **University residencies** ("becas", 3 years for EM) are accredited by **APICE** (the accreditation agency for specialty programmes) [K]. Residents are "**becados**", funded by MINSAL (with a return obligation in the public sector, "PAO", período asistencial obligatorio), by universities or self-funded [K].
- **EUNACOM** (Examen Único Nacional de Conocimientos de Medicina), created by **Ley 20.261 (2008)**, first sat in **2009** [K]: required to work in the public sector, to train as a specialist with public funding, and for foreign graduates to practise (replacing revalidation for most). Pandemic-era rules allowed temporary exceptions for foreign doctors [K, details vary].
- **Foreign doctors**: large inflows from the 2010s (Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Cuba-trained graduates, Bolivia, Peru) filled primary care and EDs, especially in the periphery. Low EUNACOM pass rates for foreign graduates were a recurring news topic [K].
- **Nurses**: university-trained **EU** (5-year degree) lead nursing care; **TENS** (2-year technical degree) do most bedside work; "paramédico" in common speech means the TENS [K].

### 6.2 Malpractice

- **Mandatory mediation** (Ley 19.966, Title III, from 2005): before suing over health harm, the patient must go through mediation: with the **Consejo de Defensa del Estado (CDE)** for public providers (free), or with a **mediator registered with the Superintendencia** for private providers [K].
- **Civil liability**: contractual or tort claims against clinics and doctors; for the State, liability for **"falta de servicio"** (service failure) under Ley 19.966 [K].
- **Criminal route**: complaints for **cuasidelito de homicidio / lesiones** (negligent homicide or injury, Código Penal arts. 490–491) are common pressure tools; prosecutors and the **Servicio Médico Legal** (SML) get involved; autopsy is mandatory for violent or suspicious deaths [K].
- Awards were modest by US standards in 2005 and rose through the 2010s; clinics buy **civil-liability insurance**; doctors rely on FALMED and private policies [K/E].
- **Patients' rights**: **Ley 20.584 (2012)** on rights and duties of patients: informed consent, access to records, dignity, complaint procedures [K].

### 6.3 Labour law and 24-hour shifts

- Private employees: **Código del Trabajo**; ordinary week **45 h** until 2024, then **44 h (Apr 2024)**, **42 h (Apr 2026)**, **40 h (Apr 2028)** under **Ley 21.561 (2023)** [K]. 24-hour shifts and "4th-shift" rotations need an **exceptional working-time system** authorized by the Dirección del Trabajo (art. 38) [K].
- **"Cuarto turno"** (common in nursing/TENS): day–night–off–off cycles of 12-h shifts [K].
- Many private ED doctors are **not employees** but bill per shift by boleta de honorarios: no paid leave, easy to drop; a constant source of tension and of lawsuits seeking recognition of employment [K/E].
- **Ley Karin (Ley 21.643, in force Aug 2024)**: workplace harassment and violence protocols, investigations, a real management burden in EDs [K].
- Assaults on health workers: penal reforms of the late 2010s increased penalties for attacks on health staff [K, number to verify].

### 6.4 The public–private split ("doble militancia")

- Most specialists **work both sides**: mornings in a public hospital (a 22- or 33-hour contract, prestige, teaching, residents), afternoons and shifts in private clinics (money) [K]. Public hospitals complain of doctors who "leave at 13:00"; clinics depend on public-trained doctors.
- **For the game**: the enterprise's doctors will often **also work at the HUAP, Sótero or Barros Luco**, which gives inside knowledge of bed status, personal favours for transfers, and conflicts of interest (diverting patients).

### 6.5 Unions and strikes

- Public sector: **FENPRUSS** (university-trained professionals), **FENATS** (technicians and workers), **CONFUSAM** (municipal primary care), **Colegio de Enfermeras**; the Colegio Médico calls national "paros" (strikes) over pay, budgets and reforms [K]. Strikes usually keep EDs running "con turnos éticos" (minimum emergency staffing) [K].
- Private sector: clinic unions (TENS, nurses) strike over pay; CLC's 2020s financial crisis brought layoffs and protests [K].

## 8. DM quick rules (summary)

1. **Nobody can be refused or asked for money first** in a vital emergency, in any year of the game. From Nov 2009, no cheque or cash guarantee for anything.
2. **The ED doctor's certificate is money**: it decides who pays. Auditors will review it months later.
3. **Stabilization is a legal moment**: certified by the treating doctor; only then can the patient be moved; for Fonasa patients it triggers the **DOMA** (from 2022 formally; earlier as an informal choice).
4. **Transfers fail far more often than they succeed** (only ~11–14% of Fonasa patients stabilized in private clinics were transferred). Plan for patients who stay.
5. **From 2015, Fonasa pays the waiting days**. Before that, every extra day is a fight.
6. **Mutual, SOAP and Isapre patients are good business**; Fonasa tramo A walk-ins are a mission, not a margin.
7. **From July 2016, accreditation** is the price of being in an Isapre's GES trauma network.
8. **2022–2024**: the Isapres are shaky, the paperwork is stricter, the press is watching the rebase.
