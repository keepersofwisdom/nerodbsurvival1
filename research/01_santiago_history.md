## 1. Colonial Santiago (1541–1810)

- **Founding.** Pedro de Valdivia founded *Santiago del Nuevo Extremo* on **12 February 1541**, on a plain bounded by the Mapocho river to the north and a dry southern arm of it (*La Cañada*, later the Alameda), at the foot of the hill the Mapuche/Picunche called **Huelén** (today Cerro Santa Lucía). The town was laid out in a **damero** (chequerboard grid) of square blocks around the **Plaza de Armas**.
- **First destruction.** On **11 September 1541** the cacique Michimalonco attacked and burned the infant town. Chroniclers celebrate Inés de Suárez in the defence. The coincidence with 11 September 1973 is a favourite of Chilean writers.
- **The colonial city** was small and low, built of adobe and tile, with church towers and convents (Santo Domingo, San Francisco, La Merced, San Agustín), the **Cabildo** (town council), and the **Real Audiencia** (royal court). It remained a frontier capital of a poor captaincy-general within the Viceroyalty of Peru.
- **Earthquakes** shaped everything:
  - **13 May 1647.** The "Great Earthquake" destroyed most of the city and killed a large share of the population (estimates vary widely). The crucifix of the **Señor de Mayo** (Cristo de Mayo), whose crown of thorns reputedly slipped to its neck, is still carried in procession every 13 May from the Iglesia de San Agustín. [Legend for the miracle; the procession is real]
  - **8 July 1730.** A great earthquake and tsunami struck the central coast, with heavy damage in Santiago.
  - Later destructive quakes: 1822 (Valparaíso), 16 Aug 1906 (Valparaíso, with damage in Santiago), 1971 (La Ligua), **3 March 1985** (central coast, M≈7.8, ≈177 dead nationally; it wrecked old adobe housing in central Santiago), and **27 Feb 2010** (see file 02).
- **Water.** The Mapocho flooded repeatedly. The **tajamares** (stone river walls) were rebuilt in the 1790s. The **Puente de Cal y Canto** (1767–1782) linked the city to La Chimba until a flood destroyed it in 1888.
- **La Moneda.** It was built 1784–1805 to designs by Italian architect **Joaquín Toesca** as the royal **mint** (*moneda* = coin). It became the presidents' residence and seat of government in 1846.
- **La Chimba** (Quechua for "the other side") was the poorer north bank. It became the city's medical and mortuary district: the **Cementerio General** (1821), Hospital San José, the **Casa de Orates** asylum (1852, later the Instituto Psiquiátrico), and later the **Instituto de Higiene** (1892, a building the CNI would use as Cuartel Borgoño; §7). ## 2. The republican city (1810–1930)

- **Independence.** The first Junta formed on **18 September 1810** (now the national holiday, *Fiestas Patrias* or *el Dieciocho*). Independence was declared on 12 Feb 1818 and secured at the **Battle of Maipú** (5 April 1818), on what is now the south-west edge of Santiago.
- **The "Portalian" state.** Diego Portales and the 1833 Constitution built a centralised, authoritarian, oligarchic republic that was unusually stable for Latin America. A landed aristocracy of Basque-Castilian families (the "*fronda aristocrática*", Alberto Edwards' 1928 phrase) dominated politics until the 1920s.
- **Mining money** from silver (Chañarcillo, 1832) and nitrates after the War of the Pacific (1879–84) built palaces on Calle Dieciocho, Ejército, República and around the Alameda. The **1891 civil war** (President Balmaceda against Congress) ended in Balmaceda's suicide in the Argentine legation.
- **Benjamín Vicuña Mackenna**, Intendente 1872–75, turned the rocky Cerro Santa Lucía into a romantic park and planned a *Camino de Cintura* (ring road) to separate the "civilised" city from the poor *arrabales* (outskirts). This was an early, explicit class map.
- **Centenary works (≈1910):** Estación Mapocho, the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes and the Parque Forestal, the Biblioteca Nacional, and the Palacio de los Tribunales. The **Barrio Cívico** around La Moneda (government blocks, Plaza de la Constitución) followed in the 1930s–40s, influenced by Austrian planner Karl Brunner.
- **The "social question"** brought *conventillos* (tenement slums), epidemics (smallpox, typhus), very high infant mortality, and strikes and massacres (Santa María school, Iquique 1907). Public medicine grew out of this: Beneficencia hospitals, the 1924 Seguro Obrero, and later the **Servicio Nacional de Salud (SNS, 1952)**, the backbone of the public system. Salvador Allende, a physician, was its intellectual co-author as health minister (1939–42).
- **Elite flight east.** From the 1900s the rich left the old centre (Dieciocho, República) for **Providencia** (comuna created 1897) and then **Las Condes** (1901). The pattern of "always further up the cone towards the mountains" is still running.

## 3. Class geography of the comunas

Santiago is one of the most spatially segregated cities in Latin America. Planners call the rich north-east wedge the **"cono de alta renta"** (high-income cone).

| Zone | Comunas | Character (c. 2005) |
|---|---|---|
| **Barrio alto (cone)** | Providencia, Las Condes, Vitacura, Lo Barnechea (La Dehesa), upper La Reina | Old and new money, embassies, private clinics (Clínica Alemana in Vitacura, Clínica Las Condes, Clínica Santa María and Indisa in Providencia), the elite Catholic and English schools, the El Golf/Isidora Goyenechea business district ("**Sanhattan**") |
| **Middle-class east/south-east** | Ñuñoa, La Reina, Macul, Peñalolén (mixed), La Florida | Professionals and public employees. Peñalolén mixes gated condos with *tomas* (squats) |
| **Centre** | Santiago, Estación Central, Independencia, Recoleta | Government, commerce, universities, old hospitals, the Posta Central, immigrant barrios (Peruvian community around the Plaza de Armas and the Catedral by the 2000s) |
| **Popular south and west** | Pudahuel, Cerro Navia, Lo Prado, Renca, Conchalí, Huechuraba (mixed), Pedro Aguirre Cerda, Lo Espejo, San Ramón, La Granja, La Pintana, El Bosque, San Bernardo, Puente Alto, Maipú (lower-middle) | Poblaciones born of 1950s–70s *tomas*. **La Victoria** (occupied 30 Oct 1957), La Legua, Lo Hermida, Villa Francia, La Pincoya and Herminda de la Victoria were the 1980s protest strongholds |

- **Comuna reform.** A 1981 decree reorganised the metropolitan comunas. Many new ones (La Pintana, Lo Espejo, Cerro Navia, Lo Prado, Pedro Aguirre Cerda, San Joaquín, Macul, Peñalolén, El Bosque, Recoleta, Independencia, Huechuraba, Estación Central) were installed during the 1980s. **Vitacura and Lo Barnechea** were split from Las Condes in **1991**. (?) The exact installation year varies by comuna.
- **Erradicaciones (1979–85).** The dictatorship forcibly removed tens of thousands of families from campamentos in the east and centre to new social housing on the southern and western periphery. This homogenised rich and poor comunas. (The figure commonly cited is ≈29,000 families; not re-verified in this compilation.)
- **1979 land-market liberalisation** declared urban land "not a scarce resource" and removed the urban limit. The 1994 *Plan Regulador Metropolitano* reimposed one. This produced sprawl: Puente Alto, Maipú and La Florida became cities of 0.5–0.8 million each.
- **Population of Greater Santiago (≈):** 1907 ≈ 0.33 M · 1940 ≈ 1 M · 1970 ≈ 2.8 M · 1982 ≈ 3.9 M · 1992 ≈ 4.8 M · 2002 census ≈ 5.4 M (Metropolitan Region ≈ 6.1 M).

## 5. Tuesday 11 September 1973, hour by hour (Santiago)

**Aftermath in the city:**
- **Estadio Nacional** (Ñuñoa) became the largest detention camp, from 11 Sept to 9 Nov 1973 ✔. The Red Cross counted **≈7,000** prisoners on its first visit (22 Sept) ✔. The camp commander told La Tercera "3,500–4,000" (23 Sept) ✔. Over the whole period, **≈20,000** prisoners from 38 countries are estimated to have passed through ✔. The stadium has dressing-room cells, the velodrome used for interrogation, and the "Escotilla 8" memorial area.
- **Estadio Chile** (downtown, now **Estadio Víctor Jara**) held students and staff seized at the UTE. The singer **Víctor Jara** was tortured and killed there in mid-September ✔. His body was found by the Cementerio Metropolitano. Ex-officers were convicted decades later [Doc].
- The **Instituto Médico Legal** (the morgue, in La Chimba) and the **Cementerio General**'s **Patio 29**, where unidentified (NN) bodies were buried in late 1973, became central to the search for the disappeared. Patio 29 was exhumed in 1991. In 2006 it emerged that many of those identifications had been wrong: a scandal at the Servicio Médico Legal (SML), the forensic service [Doc].
- In Oct 1973 the "**Caravana de la Muerte**" (General Sergio Arellano Stark's helicopter tour of provincial garrisons) killed ≈97 prisoners [Doc].

## 6. The dictatorship's machinery (1973–1990)

### 6.1 Institutions

| Body | Dates | What it was |
|---|---|---|
| **Junta de Gobierno** | 1973–1990 | Pinochet (Army), Merino (Navy), Leigh (Air Force, ousted 1978, replaced by Fernando Matthei), Mendoza (Carabineros, resigned 1985 after the *degollados* case, replaced by Rodolfo Stange). From Dec 1974 Pinochet held the executive as "President of the Republic", and the Junta acted as legislature |
| **DINA** (*Dirección de Inteligencia Nacional*) | Operated from late 1973, formally created by decree-law 521 (June 1974), dissolved Aug 1977 | Political police under Col./Gen. **Manuel Contreras**, answering to Pinochet. Ran the clandestine detention and extermination centres (§7), **Operación Cóndor** with other Southern Cone regimes, and murders abroad: General Prats in Buenos Aires (1974), the attempt on Bernardo Leighton in Rome (1975), Orlando Letelier in Washington (1976). Its headquarters are usually given as **Belgrado 11**, Santiago, near Plaza Baquedano (?). Not re-verified |
| **CNI** (*Central Nacional de Informaciones*) | Aug 1977–Feb 1990 | DINA's successor, more bureaucratic but still lethal in the 1980s: Operación Albania (1987), the killing of union leader Tucapel Jiménez (1982, carried out by army intelligence), and many "*enfrentamientos*" (staged shoot-outs). Main Santiago torture site: **Cuartel Borgoño** (§7). On dissolution its personnel and archives passed largely to the Army's intelligence directorate (DINE) |
| **Comando Conjunto** | ≈1975–76 | Air Force-led joint group (with Carabineros and Navy elements) that hunted the Communist Party. Sites included the "Nido 20" and "Nido 18" houses and the AGA |
| **SIFA/DIFA** (Air Force), **SIM/DINE** (Army), **SICAR/DICOMCAR** (Carabineros intelligence) | Various | DICOMCAR ran the 1985 "*degollados*" murders: Communist professionals José Manuel Parada, Manuel Guerrero and Santiago Nattino had their throats cut [Doc] |

**Victim figures [Doc]:** the **Rettig Commission** (report delivered Feb 1991) recognised ≈2,279 deaths and disappearances. After later bodies (the Corporación Nacional de Reparación y Reconciliación, Valech II in 2011), the State recognises **≈3,200** dead and disappeared. The **Valech Commission** (2004) recognised **27,255** survivors of political imprisonment and torture ✔ (from ≈35,000 testimonies ✔). **Valech II** (2011) added ≈9,800 more (≈). Hundreds of thousands went into exile.

### 6.2 Curfew and daily life
- A **curfew** ran from 1973 in shifting hours, typically from around midnight or 1 a.m. to dawn in the 1980s. It was finally lifted at the start of 1987 (≈). Doctors, nurses, ambulance crews and other essential workers needed **salvoconductos** (curfew passes). Being caught out after curfew meant a night in a police station, or worse.
- **States of siege** were declared repeatedly: Nov 1984 to mid-1985, and after the Sept 1986 assassination attempt.
- Informers (*sapos*) were everywhere. Phones were tapped. Opposition magazines (*Análisis*, *Cauce*, *APSI*, *Hoy*) were periodically closed. The newspaper ***El Mercurio*** (Edwards family) backed the regime. Its 1975 "*Exterminados como ratones*" headline about the Operación Colombo disappeared became notorious [Doc].

## 8. Doctors and clinics: repression and resistance

### 8.1 The DINA's clinics [Doc/Rep]
- The DINA ran **three clinics**: **Clínica Santa Lucía** (Santa Lucía 162), **Clínica London** (Almirante Barroso 76) and **Clínica El Golf** (location not confirmed in this compilation; the name suggests the El Golf district of Las Condes (?)). The staff appear in a **judicial investigation that remains archived** ✔.
- **Official cover:** health care for DINA agents and their families ✔. **Reality:** detainees injured under torture were brought in for "repair" so interrogation could continue. Testimonies describe prisoners **blindfolded and tied to beds** under constant threat ✔. A 2005 testimony describes detainee deaths at Santa Lucía after the administration of substances ✔. Survivors call it an extermination site as well ✔.
- **Staff:** CIPER (2023, "Papeles de la Dictadura") counted **at least 31 people (26 doctors, 5 nurses)** who worked in these clinics. **Only five doctors have been convicted** in DINA cases, and **at least seven were still practising** in 2023 ✔. Judge Alejandro Solís's investigation found that **some doctors monitored detainees during torture** ✔. CIPER stresses that many staff only provided services and were not necessarily involved in crimes, though all knew they worked for the DINA ✔. An anaesthetist testified (2003) that he joined Santa Lucía in 1974 for the money, not knowing at first it was a DINA barracks ✔.
- **Clínica London, 1977:** the DINA corporal **Manuel Leyton Robles** died there after being brought in "for a check-up" (cardiorespiratory arrest) ✔. Later investigations linked his death to the DINA's chemical-weapons programme (below) [Rep/Doc (?)].
- **Project Andrea** [Doc/Rep]: DINA chemist **Eugenio Berríos** produced **sarin** and other toxins in a laboratory at the **Lo Curro house** given to US-born DINA agent **Michael Townley** and his wife, the writer Mariana Callejas. (This is a different property from Pinochet's Lo Curro mansion (§11).) Berríos was spirited to Uruguay by military intelligence in 1991 and found dead on a beach there in 1995 [Doc, convictions in Chile].
- One source links Clínica London staff to Frei Montalva's treatment at Clínica Santa María [Rep, partisan source; treat cautiously].

### 8.2 Doctors in resistance
- The **Colegio Médico de Chile** (the doctors' professional association) was at first controlled by regime-friendly leadership. In the early 1980s free internal elections were allowed and the pro-regime slate was defeated ✔. Under **Dr. Juan Luis González** (surgeon, president of the Colegio) it publicly condemned medical participation in torture ✔. González also led the **Asamblea de la Civilidad**, which called a national strike against the regime ✔. He was **arrested in July 1986** under the State Security Law, released on bail in mid-August and **acquitted in Jan 1987** ✔. He received a human-rights prize from the AAAS ✔. The Colegio opened ethics proceedings (*sumarios*) against doctors linked to torture, including expulsions ✔ (dates not confirmed here). In 1983 its president told Amnesty International that it condemned participation but **could not identify the doctors involved** ✔.
- Many doctors were themselves victims: ≈ dozens of health workers were executed or disappeared after the coup. This is documented by the Colegio and the Rettig report; exact figure not re-verified.
- **Comité Pro Paz** (*Comité de Cooperación para la Paz en Chile*, Oct 1973–Dec 1975) was ecumenical, created under **Cardinal Raúl Silva Henríquez** ✔. Pinochet forced its closure.
- **Vicaría de la Solidaridad** (1 Jan 1976–31 Dec 1992) ✔. Housed in the Archbishopric beside the **Catedral** on the Plaza de Armas ✔ (the street number Plaza de Armas 444 is often given (?)). It gave legal, medical, economic and spiritual help ✔ and filed thousands of *recursos de amparo* (habeas corpus petitions), almost all rejected by the courts. First vicar: Cristián Precht ✔. Its archive (now held by the Fundación de Documentación y Archivo de la Vicaría) is a crucial source for the disappeared. Its staff were targeted: José Manuel Parada, one of the 1985 *degollados*, worked in its archive.
- **FASIC**, CODEPU and the **Agrupación de Familiares de Detenidos Desaparecidos** (AFDD) also ran medical, psychiatric and legal programmes for torture survivors.
- **Posta Central / Asistencia Pública** (Portugal 125). In the 1980s protests, wounded protesters were taken here, and police presence in the emergency room was a recurring complaint (general reporting; not re-verified).

### 8.3 The 2019 echo
In the 2019 *estallido* (file 02) the Colegio Médico was again prominent, documenting **eye injuries from police pellets**. Volunteer medics ran first-aid posts around Plaza Italia. Clinics and emergency rooms near Plaza Baquedano received the injured.

## 9. The death of Eduardo Frei Montalva (1982) and the trial

**Background.** Frei Montalva (president 1964–70) had supported the coup at first. By 1980 he was the regime's leading opponent, heading the "No" campaign against the 1980 constitution (Caupolicán theatre rally, Aug 1980). He was the man most able to unite a centre-left opposition.

For the game this is ideal ambiguous material: a famous death in a private clinic near Plaza Italia, a secret in-room autopsy, CNI informants among the staff, a missing set of organs, and a final verdict of "unproven".

## 10. Seats of power, bunkers and the underground: what is real

### 10.1 Edificio Diego Portales (the Junta's headquarters)
- Built in 1972 in record time for the **UNCTAD III** conference. It was a 22-storey tower on a horizontal block on the Alameda, near Plaza Baquedano.
- **About three months after the coup** a Junta decree made it the military government's headquarters and renamed it **Edificio Diego Portales** ✔. Until 1981 it housed the **executive and legislative powers**, and its halls became secured spaces for the Junta's decisions ✔. Pinochet's office door was **armoured with steel plates** inside and out ✔.
- **11 March 1981:** the presidency moved to the rebuilt La Moneda ✔. The Junta, as legislature, continued in the building until 1990.
- **5 March 2006:** a fire destroyed about a third of the lower block ✔. Rebuilt as the **Centro Cultural Gabriela Mistral (GAM)**, opened in **Sept 2010** ✔. The tower ("Torre Villavicencio") has been in limbo since ✔.

### 10.2 La Moneda's reconstruction and the "búnker" [Doc/Rep]
- The bombed palace was rebuilt **1974–1981** and reopened on **11 March 1981** ✔.
- **The bunker is real.** An underground office complex, the so-called "**búnker**", was built **under the Plaza de la Constitución** (the square in front of the palace's north façade) in the early 1980s, so that Pinochet and his successors could work, or escape, in case of attack ✔. Sources date the hidden **"salón-búnker"** to 1981, 1982 or 1983 ✔. It is described as a **≈190 m² amphitheatre-style meeting room** (now called the "auditorio Prieto") where **secret meetings of the military government** were held. It is the only hall under the square to survive intact, and its restoration was planned in the 2020s ✔ [Rep: La Tercera, El Mercurio via Nuevo Poder].
- **"El Hoyo"**, the 1973–74 Carabineros (SICAR) torture cellar in the same subsoil, is a **different space** (§7). It is now part of the underground car park ✔.
- **Plaza de la Ciudadanía and the Centro Cultural La Moneda** (2006, Bicentennial works on the palace's south side) added a large underground cultural centre and car park beneath the south square.
- **[Legend]** Claims of tunnels running from La Moneda to the Ministry of Defence, the Edificio Diego Portales, the Cerro Santa Lucía or the Congress building **could not be confirmed**. A ghost-hunter claims that President Arturo Alessandri used a tunnel from the **Portal Bulnes** (Plaza de Armas) to La Moneda, since sealed. This is oral testimony with no documents ✔ [Legend].

### 10.3 Documented tunnels under Santiago [Doc/Rep]
- **Remodelación San Borja service tunnels.** A network of **almost 4 km** was built with the Remodelación San Borja (Frei Montalva era, CORMU). It still carries the **hot-water and heating infrastructure** that serves the San Borja towers ✔. It gives access towards the **GAM, Barrio Lastarria and the Posta Central** ✔. The project included a district thermal plant and water plant ✔. **Guided tours were announced for August 2026** (OH! Santiago / the Cámara Chilena de la Construcción) ✔. *This is the single most useful real precedent for a game: a real, little-known, kilometre-scale service tunnel system between Plaza Italia and the Posta Central.*
- **La Chimba hospital tunnel.** In **2019** Enel (the electricity company) works on **Av. La Paz** exposed a brick tunnel, ≈10 m by the 2019 reports and ≈30 m by 2021 reports ✔, linking the University of Chile's Faculty of Odontology (Independencia) side to the **Instituto Psiquiátrico** (Recoleta) side ✔. It is dated variously to ≈1850 or ≈1900, and was perhaps part of a network linking the hospitals and the Cementerio General ✔. An archaeologist says it joined two pavilions of the old **Casa de Orates** asylum ✔. Historians are unsure whether it was a system or isolated works ✔.
- **Daniela Améstica, *Santiago Subterráneo*** (Planeta, ≈2025–26): a researcher and tour operator who has spent a decade documenting tunnels, passages and legends under the city ✔. She names La Moneda among buildings said to be connected by tunnels. She cites the Jesuit community and Congress declining to answer her questions, and the **Barrio República**, where freemasonry, the dictatorship and Belle Époque cellars intersect ✔. Most accesses are sealed, insanitary or at risk of collapse ✔. *Mixes documented and legendary material. Treat claims individually.*
- **Metro.** Line 1 (first section opened **15 Sept 1975**, San Pablo–La Moneda) runs under the Alameda past La Moneda, built by cut-and-cover. [Legend] Rumours of sealed side-galleries and government access shafts are a staple of urban folklore. No documentation was found.

### 10.4 Pinochet's own fortified properties [Rep]
- **Lo Curro** (on the Lo Curro hill above the Mapocho, Vitacura): a presidential mansion revealed by *Cauce* magazine in **Jan 1984**, while still under construction, with red marble, tennis courts and saunas, **paid from public funds** during the post-1982 crisis ✔. Described as a **fortress built on six levels, two of them entirely underground**. Building it required **dynamiting the hill and moving ≈500,000 m³ of earth** ✔. It was later used by the **Club Militar** ✔. *This is the best documented real case of a powerful man building a fortified, partly subterranean residence in Santiago in the 1980s.*
- **El Melocotón** (Cajón del Maipo, ≈1 hour south-east): Pinochet's walled weekend estate. Land acquired from 25 Jan 1979, ≈14 ha by Dec 1983, with a main house of 606 m² and later outbuildings ✔. The **7 Sept 1986 attack** took place on the road there (§11).
- Pinochet's last private home was in **La Dehesa** (Lo Barnechea), where he was held under house arrest in the 2000s.

### 13.2 What their world looks like (texture)
- **Addresses:** La Dehesa (Lo Barnechea), Santa María de Manquehue, San Damián and Lo Curro (Vitacura), Los Dominicos (Las Condes), old houses in Pedro de Valdivia Norte and Providencia's Barrio El Golf. Weekend fundos in Colchagua or Zapallar, ski chalets at Farellones or Portillo.

- **Speech and manners:** the "*cuico*" accent (Chilean slang for posh), a blend of formality and very Chilean idiom. *Usted* with servants and *tú* with peers. Many were preppers before the word: generators, water tanks and a pantry are common in barrio-alto houses after 1985 and 2010 (general observation, not a documented statistic).

## 14. Hooks for play (FICTION IDEAS, not history)

1. **The San Borja galleries.** The real ≈4 km service tunnels under the San Borja towers lead towards the Posta Central and the GAM. *Fiction:* a disused branch, walled up in 1974 "for security", runs north-east towards a private clinic on the Plaza Italia side. Its heating pipes still run warm. Someone pays the gas bill.
2. **The six-level house.** Pinochet's Lo Curro mansion really had two fully underground floors dug out of dynamited hillside. *Fiction:* in 1984–86, three of his civilian admirers hired the same engineers for their own houses. The engineers' 1985 earthquake-proofing reports turn up in a dead man's papers.
3. **The clinic with two ledgers.** The DINA ran clinics at Santa Lucía 162 and Almirante Barroso 76 under a cover of agents' health care. *Fiction:* the protagonist's newly bought building was the "third clinic". Its basement plan doesn't match the municipal file. A retired nurse, now 80, recognises the stairwell.
4. **Frei's missing organs.** The real 1982 in-room autopsy removed organs, and the 2023 verdict was "homicide not proven". *Fiction:* a sealed jar archive turns up in a private pathology lab's sub-basement. Whoever controls it can reopen a magnicide, and an old man in La Dehesa needs it never to be found.
5. **Under the square.** The real salón-búnker and the real "El Hoyo" share the subsoil of the Plaza de la Constitución. *Fiction:* the 1981 construction crews were told to leave a sealed knockout panel facing south-east, towards the Metro Line 1 works. [Legend status in-world: everybody's uncle heard about it.]
6. **Sarin in the attic.** The DINA really made sarin in a Lo Curro house (Project Andrea), and its chemist was murdered in 1995. *Fiction:* not every batch was destroyed. Ampoules in a 1970s German-labelled cold store are part of a bunker's "defence inventory", which the doctor's medical training lets her recognise.
7. **Colonia Dignidad's method.** Real tunnels and arms caches were found there in 2005. *Fiction:* the Santiago bunkers were built by the same German-speaking contractors in 1978–88, with the same doors and the same radio sets. One of them is still alive in Parral.
8. **Riggs money, concrete form.** Real offshore shells hid Pinochet-era funds. *Fiction:* the bunker trust ("Fundación Huelén") is one such shell, paid in UF-indexed instalments, with trustees who are retired generals, a Chicago-trained economist and a priest from a barrio-alto parish.
9. **The man from Providencia's town hall.** Modelled on the real phenomenon of ex-DINA men in local office. *Fiction:* a former municipal official approved the "seismic water-storage tanks" permits for three clinics in 1996–2011. The tanks are 40 m deep.
10. **Curfew ambulances.** Real curfew passes let ambulances move at night from 1973 to 1987. *Fiction:* one private ambulance company's night runs in 1984–86 carried no patients. Its old garage (Providencia, near the river) has a ramp that goes down further than any parking.
11. **Patio 29 and the wrong bodies.** The real 2006 SML misidentification scandal. *Fiction:* one family's "identified" father was never in Patio 29. His dental chart matches a skeleton in a sealed room under a clinic.
12. **The Hospital of La Chimba.** The real Av. La Paz tunnel linked asylum pavilions. *Fiction:* the CNI used Cuartel Borgoño (also real, also in La Chimba, an 1892 hygiene institute) to reach the old network. Plans exist in the Ministerio de Bienes Nacionales archive, misfiled under "drainage".
13. **Plaza Italia, October 2019.** Real: weeks of battles at Plaza Baquedano, wounded taken to nearby clinics. *Fiction:* bunker owners met underground during the curfew while the city burned above. Someone recorded it, and in 2025 the recording reaches the young heir.
14. **The "Interferencia secreta" tapes.** The real radio recordings of 11 Sept 1973 survive. *Fiction:* there is a second, unpublished reel from a private relay station on a barrio-alto rooftop. On it, a civilian voice gives orders.
15. **Old men and their doctors.** Real: Pinochet was arrested in a London private clinic, and Frei died in a Santiago one. *Fiction:* the protagonist's first VIP patient, an ex-minister, comes to the clinic at night for a cardiac event because "my own clinic has too many eyes", and pays in gold Chilean pesos minted in the 1970s.
